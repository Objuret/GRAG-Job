## What exists before a question comes

**The graph.** The arm reads it and never writes to it.

- **Chunks:** 4,869. A chunk carries the vector of its description, its kind of record and its position in its file. It carries no text; the text is in the corpus file it points to.
- **Tags:** each has a name and a vector.
- **Links:** one from a chunk to each of its tags, carrying five values: topic, temporal, why, activity, concreteness.
  - Topic is the cosine of the tag's vector and the chunk description's vector.
  - The other four are each link's average over 24 refits of the pairwise round, written into the graph on 10-06.
- **Structure:** each chunk hangs under its file and one product, and a Slack chunk under its channel. Products and kinds have names. Channels, employees and customers have an id and a pointer.
- **The facet layer node:** one gap per facet. Temporal 1.01, why 1.38, activity 1.06, concreteness 1.30.

## Once, when a run starts (about 70 seconds)

1. **Reads the pool into memory.**
   - Chunks with a description vector, at least one link and a product: 4,808. The 61 chunks of the three metadata files have no product and are out.
   - Tags with a vector and a link into that pool: 16,654.
   - The links between them with their five values: 61,018.
   - The 30 product names, each chunk's product and channels, and the facet layer node.
2. **Checks the layer.** Every link must carry the five values, and their count and hash must match the layer node. Topic is worked out again from the two vectors, and the stored value must equal it.
3. **Takes out the tags named like a product:** 45. That leaves 16,609 tags and 57,204 links. The chain runs on those.
4. **Works out neighbours** from the chunks' positions, by kind of record:
   - Slack: same channel, and the message ranges overlap or touch.
   - Parts of one document or transcript: same record, and the character ranges overlap or touch.
   - Runs of whole records (pull requests, urls, chats): the ranges must overlap.
   
   That gives 2,787 pairs. Chunks that hang together through neighbours form a near group: 402 groups holding 3,189 chunks. The code itself marks this rule as the agent's construction from 09-13, not your ruling.
5. **Works out the "same thing" level.** It takes the pairs of tags whose names are equal once lower-cased and with a final s dropped from each word longer than three letters: 1,955 pairs. The level is the cosine that 5% of those pairs lie under: 0.9212.
6. **Works out two things per link:**
   - its place per facet: its rank among all 57,204 links, as a share;
   - its class per facet: how many gaps it lies below that facet's largest value. The gap is the layer node's.
7. **Builds the tree** as text: each product, under it its kinds of record, and under "slack threads" its channels. 30 products, 527 places. Channel names are read from the corpus file through the pointers.

After this the database is not touched again.

## Per question

**1. First call to Sonnet.** It gets the instruction and the question between markers, with no tree. The answer is forced into a schema. It returns:
- a description, 1 to 3 sentences, of the content the question is looking for;
- tags of that content, with names of people, products and channels kept out;
- tags from the question's own words;
- five readings from 0 to 1 on every tag. For a content tag: how relevant it is to the described content, seen through each facet. For a question tag: how relevant it is to the question itself.

A phrase repeated within a list is dropped. The answer is saved under the exact request and read from there next time. An unusable answer is asked once more.

**2. Second call to Sonnet.** It gets the tree, then the task, and as marked parts the question, the description and the phrases of both tag lists without their readings. It returns places as paths in the tree.

**3. Embedding,** on this laptop, one text at a time. The description and every tag phrase are embedded as passages, the mode the graph's tags and chunk descriptions were embedded in. The raw question is embedded as a query. A text's vector is kept on disk, so a text is embedded once.

**4. The chain.** Both tag lists count as query tags, around ten on a question. A phrase standing in both lists is two query tags.

For each query tag:

1. **Closeness** to every graph tag: the cosine of the two vectors.
2. **Standing.** The middle is the median of those cosines. The spread is 1.4826 times the median distance from the middle. A tag's standing is (cosine − middle) / spread, and below 0 counts as 0.
3. **The same thing.** The graph tags at or above 0.9212 count as the same thing as the query tag. Their links are put in order by these keys:
   - the link's class in the facet where the query tag's reading is largest, then in its second, third and fourth facet. Only temporal, why, activity and concreteness take part, and only the order of the readings is used, not their size. The topic reading is never used.
   - then topic on the link, higher first;
   - then closeness, higher first;
   - then chunk id and tag.
   
   In that order the links get new closenesses, spread evenly from the closest tag's closeness down towards 0.9212. Every other link keeps its tag's closeness.
4. **Fit of a link:** (its closeness − middle) / spread, and below 0 counts as 0. No facet value multiplies it.
5. **A chunk keeps its best link.** A chunk with no link gets 0.

Across the query tags:

6. **Weight of a query tag:** its cosine to the description, divided by the largest such cosine among the question's tags. A negative one counts as 0.
7. **Tag strength of a chunk:** the largest, over the query tags, of weight times the chunk's best fit. The query tag that gives it is the chunk's winner, and that tag's best link is the winning link.

The text side:

8. The description's vector against every chunk description, and the raw question's vector against every chunk description. Each becomes a standing over all 4,808 chunks, as in step 2 but not cut at 0. **Text strength** is the larger of the two.

Together:

9. **Strength** = tag strength + text strength.
10. **Product lift:** the average strength of the other chunks of the chunk's product, minus the average over all chunks. Below 0 counts as 0.
11. **Near lift,** for a chunk in a near group. Take every chunk's strength minus its product's average. The lift is the average of that over the group's other chunks, minus the average of that over all grouped chunks of the same kind of record. Below 0 counts as 0.
12. **Final strength** = strength + product lift + near lift.

The order:

13. **Levels.** One level is 0.002 of closeness divided by the median spread of the query tags. A chunk's level is how many whole levels it lies below the strongest chunk.
14. **Inside a level:**
    - chunks with a link come before chunks without;
    - then by the place of the chunk's winning link in the facet its winning query tag reads highest, then that tag's second, third and fourth facet;
    - then by chunk id.
    
    A chunk whose tag strength is 0 still gets a winner: the first query tag and the chunk's first-listed link. Its tie-break comes from that arbitrary link.

So the facets act at two points only: they order the links of the same-thing tags in step 3, and they break ties inside a level in step 14.

**5. Scope.** Of each returned place only the product is used. All chunks of those products move to the front, in the chain's order among themselves. The rest follow in the chain's order. Nothing is removed.

**6. Delivery.** Going down the order, each chunk's text is fetched from its corpus file through its locator, and the file is checked against the hash the graph holds. Whole chunks are added until 72,000 characters, and the chunk that crosses the line is cut mid-text. That cut is the harness's. The cut chunk's text is delivered, but it is not counted as delivered in the scores.

**7. Answer and scoring,** by the harness. In a full run Sonnet gets "Answer the question using only the provided documents. Be concise.", the delivered texts and the question, and Haiku judges. In a retrieval-only run there is no answer, and only the scores that need no model are made.

- **Why:** it means the same thing on both routes, and nothing in it is chosen by me. There is no range, no unit and no cut-off; it comes from the chunks' own order.
- **Cost:** it says how rare a number is, not how near in meaning. More on that at the end.

**Step 2. Take the number of tags out of the tags' number.** The tags' number is the best of all the chunk's tags, so a chunk with many tags gets many tries at one good number. Today chunks with 16 to 48 tags are in the best tenth 2.7 times as often as chunks with 1 to 8 tags.

- **What I do:** look up how good the chunk's best link is among all 57,204 links, say 1 in 1,000. Then ask how often the best of that chunk's tries would be that good by luck: about 6 in 1,000 for a chunk with 6 tags, about 22 in 1,000 for one with 22.
- **Why:** that is the exact chance for a best-of-many. It is also your rule that the amount of tags must not make a chunk more important.
- **Result:** from few tags to many, the four groups were in the best tenth 5.0%, 5.7%, 9.3% and 13.3% of the time. Now they are 10.8%, 7.7%, 10.0% and 12.2%.
- **Cost:** tags that mean nearly the same are not separate tries, so it takes out slightly too much.

**Step 3. Multiply the two fractions.** 1 in 100 on the tags and 1 in 10 on the description is 1 in 1,000 on both. The smaller it is, the less it can be luck. The chunks are ordered by that.

- **Why multiply:** over the whole pool the two numbers have nothing to do with each other (correlation −0.01). Luck on two unrelated things at once is one chance times the other.
- **Upside:** it does both things you asked of the two routes. A chunk that is extreme on one route still floats, which is the width. A chunk that is good on both floats more, which is the reinforcing.
- **Result:** 66 of today's first 100 stay.

**Why not the other ways of putting them together.** Where the first 100 chunks come from:

| Way | Tags only | Description only | Both |
|---|---|---|---|
| Added as they are (today) | 3 | 56 | 41 |
| The two fractions multiplied | 23 | 22 | 55 |
| The two positions added | 16 | 8 | 76 |
| Only the better of the two | 31 | 37 | 32 |

Adding positions demands that both routes agree, so the width is lost. It also counts a step in the middle of the pack like a step at the top. Taking only the better one gives width and no gain from reinforcing. Multiplying sits between them.

**One thing the calculation showed.** At the top the two routes agree far more than luck: 17 chunks are in the best 2% on both, where luck gives 1.9.

**What it costs, and what is yours.**

- **It forgets how far ahead the best is.** On the description route there is always a best 1%, whether anything truly fits or not. On the tag route this is partly kept: all querytags' links are compared in one list, so a querytag with no real match lands lower. Today's sum keeps the nearness itself.
- **Both routes get the same say.** You have said the tags are the main path. A weight for that would be a number picked by me.
- **Documents stay out either way.** None of the 256 is in the first 100, because the description's number puts whole kinds of record high or low.

Two things from the check that bear on the thesis:

- **Nobody was found doing this in text retrieval.** Turning each score into a chance and multiplying them is published for sequence and protein search (Bailey & Gribskov 1998, Alves et al. 2008).
